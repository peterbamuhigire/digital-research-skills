# Dissertation Unwritten Conventions

Use this guide when planning, drafting or reviewing a doctoral dissertation or research proposal.
It covers the conventions examiners assume but rarely state: how the project is managed, how the
supervisory relationship works, what each chapter must prove, and how findings are reported.
Formal reporting standards (PRISMA, CONSORT, STROBE and others) live in `equator-decision-tree.md`.

## Inputs

- Institution, faculty and degree regulations (word limit, format, examination model).
- Supervisor or chair and committee membership, and their recent graduates' dissertations.
- Research question, design and data plan.
- Timeline, funding and ethics approval status.

## Decision rules

1. **Treat the dissertation as proof of capability, not a final exam.** Examiners ask whether the
   candidate can conduct independent scholarly work, not whether they remember coursework.
2. **The candidate initiates contact.** Supervisors read silence as progress. Book meetings,
   send short written updates, and restate agreed actions in writing afterwards.
3. **Choose a supervisor on completion record, not prestige.** Ask how many doctoral students
   they have seen through (count, not percentage), typical time to completion, and where
   graduates went; talk to current and former students; agree working style early. A first-time
   supervisor is an accepted risk, not a disqualification.
4. **Work in parallel.** While the committee holds chapters 1 to 3, draft chapter 4. Never wait
   idle for feedback.
5. **Approval is collective and can be withdrawn.** A supervisor-approved draft can still fail
   with external examiners; plan revision time.
6. **The approved proposal is a working contract.** It binds scope; regulations and staff can
   change, so do not let the project drift for years.
7. **Do not stall at "all but dissertation".** Set a finish date and protect it.
8. **Read the genre before writing it.** Read three to five recent dissertations supervised by
   your committee, cover to cover.
9. **Manufacture community.** Attrition tracks isolation more than ability; join or form a
   writing group that meets weekly.
10. **Statistical significance is not practical significance.** Report both and keep the research
    question in charge.
11. **Pilot everything:** instruments, recording and transcription, analysis scripts.
12. **Expect recursion.** Reading, writing, analysis and revision loop; linear plans fail.

## Procedure: what each part must prove

| Part | Must establish | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Title | Scope at a glance | Variables and population for hypothesis-testing studies; focus phrase for exploratory ones; revise throughout |
| Chapter 1: problem | The study is worth doing and the candidate can do it | The problem in the discipline; theoretical rationale; potential significance; feasibility |
| Chapter 2: literature and theory | Command of the field and a clear gap | Current and seminal work organised by theme, not by author; links to the study; a sharpened contribution claim |
| Chapter 3: method | The study can be replicated and each choice is justified | Data, collection procedure, analysis procedure, validity and reliability, pilot evidence, sample justification, ethics approval, limitations |
| Chapter 4: findings | What the data shows, without overreach | Tables and figures, then narrative, then synthesis; raw evidence (excerpts, scores) so readers can judge; every inference traceable to data |
| Chapter 5: conclusions | What was learned and what follows | Summary; conclusions on the theory; implications revisiting the proposal's significance claim; a few targeted recommendations |
| Abstract | The whole study in about 350 words (check local limit) | Title focus, problem, framework, data, analysis, outcomes |

For each methodological choice, prepare the rationale in terms of the theory or phenomenon under
study; load `methodology-justification-checklist.md`. Findings are judged as thorough, clear,
logical, relevant and cautious; load `findings-interpretation-criteria.md`.

## Literature review practice

Start a topic file with the first course paper and harvest it. Record the full bibliographic
entry before reading beyond the title. Organise by theme where the field permits. Aim the review
at one of three jobs: fill a gap, resolve an inconsistency, or clarify a conflict between
theories. Connect to the committee's expertise. Stop broad reading when new sources cite only
works you know; return for targeted reading when drafting exposes a gap.

## Working routine

Block daily undisturbed writing time and write every day. Break chapters into sections and
paragraphs and tick them off. Set personal deadlines and meet them. Use a critical reader as
devil's advocate. Keep three backups (local, external, off-site). When stuck, switch to another
sub-task or return to data displays; stuckness often signals readiness for the next section.
Avoid major life changes mid-project where possible.

## Failure modes

Treating the dissertation as a long term paper · waiting silently for feedback · choosing a
supervisor for prestige · outsourcing statistics without owning the logic · confusing
statistical with practical significance · over-generalising · under-claiming (refusing to
interpret) · burying the main contribution in detail · saving everything for one "complete"
submission · reading critique as personal attack.

## Original worked example

A Makerere doctoral candidate in public health has chapters 1 to 3 with the committee for six
weeks. Following the parallel-work rule, she drafts the findings chapter from her pilot and
first-wave data, sends her supervisor a one-paragraph written update every two weeks, and
books the committee meeting herself. When an external examiner later questions her sampling,
her method chapter already contains the rationale and pilot evidence, so the revision takes days,
not months.

## Checklist

- [ ] Regulations, word limit and examination model confirmed.
- [ ] Supervisor chosen on completion record; working style agreed in writing.
- [ ] Three to five recent local dissertations read.
- [ ] Each chapter proves what the table requires.
- [ ] Every method choice has a written rationale; pilot completed.
- [ ] Findings trace to data; significance claims distinguish statistical and practical.
- [ ] Writing group, routine and backups in place.
