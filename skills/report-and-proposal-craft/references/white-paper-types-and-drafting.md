# White Paper Types and Drafting

Use this guide when the engine produces a white paper: a persuasive, evidence-backed long-form
document that helps business readers understand an issue, solve a problem, or make a decision.
If a draft does none of the three, it is a brochure or a blog post and should be labelled as such.
Proposal-style persuasion lives in `report-and-proposal-structure.md`.

## Inputs

- Sponsor, audience (executive, technical, influencer) and buying stage.
- The single takeaway sentence and the single call to action.
- Evidence dossier: independent data, primary survey if any, expert and customer interviews,
  quantified outcomes, all with source IDs and dates.
- Sector content norms (regulated sectors may bar comparative claims).

## Decision rule: pick one type

| Type | Buying stage | Job | Typical length | Where the sponsor appears |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Backgrounder | Late (evaluation) | Explain how one offering, standard or method works and why it fits | 6 to 8 pages | Throughout |
| Numbered list | Early (awareness) | A scannable set of warnings, tips or questions with a fresh angle | 5 to 7 pages | Close only |
| Problem/solution | Middle (consideration) | Educate on a problem and a category of solution | 8 to 12 pages | About section and call to action only |

One paper carries one type. In a problem/solution paper the product is not named before page 2;
naming it earlier turns it into a brochure.

## Two readers per paper

The skimmer reads title, subtitle, summary, headings, callouts, captions and conclusion and must
be able to reconstruct the argument from those alone. The deep reader reads everything, checks
the footnotes and forwards the paper. Write the subtitle to state audience and type ("A
problem/solution paper for finance leaders in East African microfinance").

## Structure by type

### Backgrounder

Cover · summary · the offering and the business problem · market context (standards,
regulation, trends) · how it works (diagram plus plain walkthrough) · capabilities mapped to
buyer pains · specifications and integrations · standards and compliance · deployment and
operations · two or three quantified use cases · summary · about the sponsor · one call to action.

### Numbered list

Cover with the number in the title · short introduction (under one page) · items, each a bold
heading with one to three paragraphs and a data point or example · conclusion naming the pattern
across items · short sponsor note · call to action (often the problem/solution paper).
Brainstorm fifteen to twenty items and ship the strongest seven or eight.

### Problem/solution

Cover (problem-led title) · summary · industry context and why now, no product · the problem:
drivers, who feels it, cost of inaction · current approaches and a fair account of their limits ·
a better approach described as a category · buyer's selection criteria as a checklist or table ·
proof (independent data, customer outcomes, benchmarks, expert voices) · implementation
considerations · conclusion · about the sponsor · call to action.

## Opening

The first 100 words answer "why keep reading now" and, for list and problem/solution papers,
contain no sponsor or product. Good openings: a surprising independent statistic; a three-to-five
sentence case; a question the reader cannot dismiss; a regulator's or analyst's statement; a
before-and-after turning point; the cost of inaction in money. Weak openings: "In today's
fast-paced environment", company history, a product announcement, a dictionary definition.

## Procedure

1. **Plan.** Confirm type, audience, stage, takeaway sentence and call to action in a one-page
   brief the sponsor signs.
2. **Research.** Build the fact sheet with footnotes before drafting.
3. **Write.** Body first, summary last; draft each section in one sitting; do not edit while
   drafting.
4. **Review in fixed order.** Subject expert for accuracy, editor for structure and voice,
   sponsor for strategic fit.
5. **Design.** Lay out, add visuals, proof the final on paper.
6. **Distribute.** Landing page, gating decision, and derivative pieces.

## Evidence rules

Rank evidence from strongest to weakest: independent analyst or research-firm data;
peer-reviewed or official statistics (for East Africa: national statistics bureaus, central
banks, regulators); an original survey with disclosed method and adequate sample; named customer
results with numbers; named expert interviews; trade press; the sponsor's own benchmarks (only
with method, never as sole proof); anonymous quotes (colour, not proof).

A problem/solution paper needs at least one independent figure, one official or peer-reviewed
dataset, two named expert or customer voices, and one quantified outcome. Footnote every claim
that could be challenged; flag or replace statistics older than three years; never write
"studies show" without the study.

## Voice

Useful first, persuasive second: if a competitor could put its logo on the paper and it would
still be mostly true, the framing is right. Third person outside the about section and call to
action. Strike "leading", "best-in-class", "world-class", "revolutionary", "cutting-edge",
"seamless", "robust", "synergy" and "leverage" as a verb. Numbers, names and dates instead of
adjectives. Average sentence around 17 words.

## Visuals and length

Roughly one visual per one or two pages in backgrounders and problem/solution papers; each with a
title, caption and source; one idea per chart; no 3-D, no stock images of handshakes or globes.
Pull quotes come from third parties, never the sponsor's executives.

| Type | Pages | Summary length |
|---|---|---|
| Backgrounder | 6 to 8 | 150 to 200 words |
| Numbered list | 5 to 7 | optional, 100 to 150 words |
| Problem/solution | 8 to 12 (at most 15) | 200 to 250 words |

## Failure modes

Brochure in disguise · unsupported superlatives · mixed types without control · weak opening ·
no independent proof · straw-man alternatives · several calls to action · wall of text · neutral
body then a sales pitch in the conclusion · stale statistics · anonymous everything · missing
subtitle · absolute promises ("eliminates", "guarantees").

## Original worked example

A Nairobi payments company wants a problem/solution paper on reconciliation failures in
mobile-money collections for East African SACCOs.
- Subtitle: "A problem/solution paper for SACCO finance managers in Kenya and Uganda."
- Opening: the share of member contributions that cannot be matched to a member account on the
  day received, from the regulator's or an independent survey's published figure (with source).
- The better-approach section describes automated reference matching as a category and gives a
  selection checklist; the sponsor appears only in the about section and the call to action
  (a reconciliation self-assessment).

## Ship checklist

- [ ] Type stated in the subtitle and matched by the structure.
- [ ] Paper helps the reader understand, solve or decide.
- [ ] First 100 words free of sponsor and product (list and problem/solution).
- [ ] One call to action.
- [ ] Every load-bearing claim footnoted; evidence mix met.
- [ ] Visual budget met; every visual stands alone with its source.
- [ ] Summary stands alone, under one page, written last.
- [ ] Strike-list words removed; page count in range.
