# Thesis Topic, Sources and Quotation

Use this guide when the engine plans or drafts a thesis, dissertation or long scholarly
argument. It covers four tasks: admitting and narrowing a topic, building the working
bibliography and note system, planning the argument with a provisional table of contents, and
quoting and paraphrasing safely. Examination conventions and chapter templates live in
`../../academic-reporting-standards/references/dissertation-unwritten-conventions.md`.

## Inputs

- Candidate topic, degree level, discipline and word limit.
- Languages the writer can read; sources physically and legally accessible.
- Methods the writer has actually practised.
- Supervisor, institution and citation-style requirements.

## Task 1: admit and narrow the topic

**Admission gate.** Admit a topic only if all four hold:
1. It builds on the writer's prior study or experience.
2. The core sources can be reached (archives open, datasets licensable, informants reachable).
3. The sources can be read and understood (language, technical level).
4. The method is one the writer has practised or can learn within the timeline.

If any fails, narrow or change the topic. A thesis the writer cannot finish is not a thesis.

**Narrowing.** Move from field to topic to angle to working question. Keep two titles: a
registrar-safe public title and a private working title written as the exact question. Prefer a
focused study of one case, author, period or population over a survey of everything. Turn
abstract topics ("Freedom") into situated ones ("How Uganda's 1995 Constitution defines freedom
of association").

**Scholarly-contribution test.** The output (1) addresses a defined, publicly recognisable
object; (2) says something not yet said, or re-reads it from a new angle; (3) is useful to later
researchers; (4) gives readers what they need to verify or refute it.

## Task 2: build the bibliography and note system

**Source classes.**

| Class | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Original documents, datasets, critical editions, your fieldwork | The centre of the thesis |
| Translation | Translated editions | Supporting; check key passages in the original |
| Anthology or reader | Collected excerpts | Orientation only |
| Secondary | Books and articles about the object | Interpretation; never a substitute for the primary |

**Never cite a source through another source when the original is reachable.** When it is not,
make responsibility clear: "Smith (2010, p. 56), as quoted in Otieno (2019, p. 45)".

**Search order.** Subject catalogues and discipline databases; reference works and indexes;
bibliographies of the best recent works (the most-cited items form the first tier); annotated
bibliographies; a subject librarian; union catalogues and interlibrary loan; recent-acquisition
lists. Then run `citation-brachiation.md` and `pearl-growing.md`.

**Coverage rule.** Cover the centre of the thesis completely; the periphery can be lighter. A
long bibliography with thin reading notes signals a thesis that stalled.

**Note system.** Keep separate, cross-referenced records:

| Record | Holds |
|---|---|
| Bibliographic | One per work, every field the citation style needs, location or DOI |
| Reading | Summary, verbatim quotes with page numbers in quotation marks, paraphrase without them, own comments in brackets |
| Theme | Cross-source points on one theme, each with source and page |
| Quote bank | Candidate quotations tagged by theme |
| Connection | Unresolved links and what to read next |
| Decisions | Rejected leads, open questions |

Tag each record with the outline section it serves (for example `4.2`) so notes and plan stay
linked. The engine's note-card format is in `source-away-note-cards.md`.

## Task 3: plan with a provisional table of contents

Before drafting chapters, write three things: the working title, a provisional table of contents,
and a draft introduction written as if the work were finished. The table of contents is a
research instrument. It should cover: state of the question, previous research, hypothesis or
argument, evidence, analysis, demonstration, conclusions and further research.

Expect to rewrite it many times; if it never changes, the research produced nothing new. The
final introduction should promise less, and more precisely, than the first draft.

## Task 4: quote and paraphrase safely

**Quotation rules.**
1. Quote the primary object generously enough for readers to judge your reading.
2. Quote secondary literature only where its authority supports your point or you are
   contesting it.
3. When quoting a view you do not share, say so before or after.
4. Every quotation names author and source unambiguously, with page.
5. Quote from the authoritative edition and say which edition.
6. For literary or legal primary texts in another language, quote the original and translate
   (in text or note), following the discipline's convention.
7. Short quotations run in the text in quotation marks; long ones are block-indented per the
   style guide.
8. Quote exactly: ellipsis for omissions, square brackets for insertions, [sic] for source
   errors, "emphasis added" where you add it.
9. Treat each quotation as testimony: it must be locatable and its source credible.

**Paraphrase tests.** An honest paraphrase is usually shorter than the original; it can be
written with the source closed; and where a phrase truly belongs to the source, it goes in
quotation marks. A footnote does not cure copied wording.

**Citation system.** Notes-and-bibliography suits history, law and heterogeneous sources;
author-date suits most social and natural sciences and survives late edits. Pick the one the
institution requires and apply it consistently (`citation-styles.md`).

## Prose rules for theses

Short sentences; repeat the subject rather than stacking pronouns. The thesis is about its object,
not an imitation of it: a study of experimental poetry is written in plain analytical prose.
Define every central term; if you cannot, stop and define it before continuing. Start a new
paragraph at each step in the argument. No exclamation marks, scare quotes, or explained irony.
Use the voice the discipline expects. Read widely and respectfully while researching (minor
authors often hold the best ideas); write with authority once you know the material, without
apologetic prefaces.

## Failure modes

A survey where a focused study would work · an abstract "first principles" topic · sources in a
language the writer cannot read · skipping the provisional table of contents · notes split
between files and memory · photocopying or downloading instead of reading · indirect quotation
presented as direct · unread works padding the bibliography · long, ornate sentences · "ibid."
across an intervening citation · paraphrase with a few words swapped and a footnote added.

## Original worked example

Candidate topic: "Land conflict in Uganda."
- Gate: the writer reads English and Luganda, has done qualitative interviews, and can reach
  Mukono district land-board records. Admitted, but too broad.
- Narrowed working question: "How did bona fide occupants in Mukono use the eviction protections of the Land
  (Amendment) Act 2010 in disputes filed between 2012 and 2020?"
- Primary centre: land-board records and interviews; secondary: legal commentary and land-tenure
  studies.
- Provisional table of contents written in week 2; rewritten in weeks 9 and 20 as the records
  showed most disputes were settled through clan mediation, not the board.

## Checklist

- [ ] Topic passes the four-part gate; working question written.
- [ ] Contribution test met.
- [ ] Primary sources at the centre; indirect citations marked.
- [ ] Note system cross-referenced to the outline.
- [ ] Provisional table of contents and draft introduction exist and have been revised.
- [ ] Quotations exact, located and attributed; paraphrases pass the closed-source test.
- [ ] One citation system applied consistently.
