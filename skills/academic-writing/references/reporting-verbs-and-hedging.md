# Reporting Verbs and Hedging

Use this guide when introducing a cited source (which verb signals your stance) and when stating
your own claims (how much certainty the evidence allows). Both are calibration tasks: the words
must match the strength of the evidence. For section-level moves, load
`academic-rhetorical-moves.md`; for register more broadly, `voice-and-register.md`.

## Inputs

- The claim, its source, and the source's design (experiment, survey, case, opinion).
- Whether the claim is replicated, single-study, contested or speculative.
- Your own position on the cited work: neutral, provisional, endorsing or critical.

## Decision rule 1: choose the reporting verb by stance

| Stance | Use when | Verbs |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral | Reporting background without judging it | states, describes, reports, notes, finds, examines, identifies |
| Tentative | The claim is partial, contested or one of several accounts | suggests, proposes, indicates, hypothesises |
| Endorsing | The claim is well evidenced and you accept it | demonstrates, establishes, shows, confirms |
| Positional | The author takes a side you are reporting, not endorsing | argues, contends, maintains, claims |
| Critical | You or others dispute it | questions, challenges, disputes, overlooks, fails to account for |

Calibration errors to catch:

| Error | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Strong verb on weak evidence | "Okello (2021) proves that ..." for a correlational survey | "Okello (2021) finds an association between ..." |
| Tentative verb on settled fact | "It has been suggested that malaria is transmitted by mosquitoes" | State it plainly, cited to an authority |
| One stance throughout | Every source "states" | Vary with the evidence; reserve endorsing verbs |
| No critical verbs in a contested field | Review lists positions without adjudicating | Use critical verbs where the evidence supports disagreement |
| "Claims" used for neutral reporting | Implies doubt you did not intend | Use "reports" or "states" |

## Decision rule 2: set hedge intensity by evidence strength

| Evidence | Hedge | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Strong and replicated | None | "X increases Y." |
| Strong, single study | Light | "X appears to increase Y." / "The results indicate ..." |
| Suggestive | Moderate | "The findings suggest ..." / "There is some evidence that ..." |
| Tentative | Strong | "It is possible that ..." / "These early results point towards ..." |
| Speculative | Strongest, with a caveat | "One interpretation, untested here, is ..." |

Hedge once per claim. Stacked hedges ("may possibly suggest") read as evasion; no hedges on
uncertain claims read as overconfidence. Both are examiner red flags.

## Hedging devices (engine-written patterns)

- **Distancing** (reporting a view without owning it): "It is widely assumed that ..."; "A
  common view holds that ..."; "Several studies report ..."
- **Cautious explanation of results**: "This difference may reflect [factor]."; "A likely
  explanation is [mechanism], although [alternative] remains possible."
- **Cautious implication**: "These findings point to a need for ..."
- **Limiting generalisation**: "In most of the cases studied ..."; "Among urban respondents ..."
  Use frequency words (usually, often, rarely) that match the data.
- **Future**: pick one of may, is likely to, will probably, will almost certainly, and match it
  to the evidence. For formal forecasts, pair the words with numeric bands using
  `../../analytic-tradecraft/references/estimative-probability-language.md`.
- **Own limitations**: "With a sample of 42, these results should not be generalised to all
  districts."; "The findings hold for the period studied; later policy changes are outside
  scope."

## Reuse guardrail

Short generic frames may be reused as scaffolding: roughly nine words or fewer, at most four
generic content words, no distinctive construction, and no one else's viewpoint. Anything longer
or distinctive is paraphrased from notes or quoted and cited. This rule sits alongside
`plagiarism-prevention.md`.

## Style failure modes

Personal pronouns where the discipline does not license them · contractions · rhetorical
questions as openers · everyday register ("a lot of", "get rid of") · over-generalisation ·
heavy nominalisation · formulaic repetition of the same frame · uniform hedging regardless of
evidence.

## Original worked example

Draft: "Namusoke (2022) proves that mobile-money access reduces poverty in rural Uganda."
Check: single cross-sectional survey, one region, self-reported income.
Revision: "Namusoke (2022) finds that households in Kamuli district with mobile-money accounts
report higher incomes; the cross-sectional design leaves the direction of causation open."
The verb moves from endorsing to neutral, the scope is named, and the one limitation that
matters is stated.

## Checklist

- [ ] Each reporting verb matches your stance and the source's evidence.
- [ ] Each of your claims carries a hedge matched to evidence strength, and only one.
- [ ] Critical verbs used where the literature genuinely disagrees.
- [ ] Forecasts use calibrated terms, with numeric bands where decision-grade.
- [ ] Limitations stated in plain terms.
- [ ] Reused frames within the reuse guardrail.
