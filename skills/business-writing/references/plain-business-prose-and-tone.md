# Plain Business Prose and Tone

Sentence-level and tone rules for business prose, plus channel-specific patterns. Load after the
outline exists (`business-writing-process.md`, stage 4) and during the sentence and word
revision passes. Hard scenarios such as declines and apologies have their own library in
`scenario-playbooks.md`.

## Inputs

- The draft and its one-sentence purpose.
- The reader's relationship to the writer (stranger, client, peer, senior, regulator).
- The channel.
- Any legal or regulatory exposure (complaints, disputes, employment matters).

## Core decision rules

1. **Say it at full strength.** State the point plainly in the first sentence that needs it.
   Softened, tiptoeing openings invite the reader to stop reading. Hedge only where the evidence
   is genuinely uncertain, and then once.
2. **Lead with the news.** First sentence of the document, section and paragraph carries the
   point.
3. **Specific beats vague.** Replace "significant growth" with "18% growth"; replace "recently"
   with the date; replace "stakeholders" with the named group.
4. **Short and familiar.** Short paragraphs, sentences averaging under 20 words with variety,
   and the everyday word over the formal one.
5. **Active and personal.** Name who acts. "We decided" rather than "it was decided".
6. **Understate.** "Sales grew 12%" persuades more than "we revolutionised sales".
7. **Write for what the reader does not know.** Define terms on first use; supply the context
   you live in and they do not.
8. **Make it unmisreadable.** Ambiguity is the writer's defect. Check word pairs that are easy
   to confuse (affect/effect, imply/infer, comprise/compose).
9. **Get it exactly right.** A misspelt name or a wrong figure costs more credibility than any
   style gain.
10. **Plain language survives technical subjects.** Complexity is a reason for clarity, not an
    excuse for fog.

## Word-level substitution (engine list)

Default: cut or replace these whenever the plain form works.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| utilise, leverage (verb) | use |
| facilitate | help, run |
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that, in light of the fact that | because |
| at this point in time, going forward | now, from now (or cut) |
| please be advised that, kindly note that | (cut; state the fact) |
| attached please find | I have attached, here is |
| we are in receipt of | we have received |
| at your earliest convenience | by [date] |
| revert (meaning reply) | reply |
| impact (verb) | affect, change |
| operationalise | put into practice |
| synergy, win-win, paradigm shift, value-added | name the actual gain |
| pursuant to, heretofore, the aforementioned | under, until now, this |

Test any dense paragraph by rewriting it as a respected plain-spoken investor letter would. If
the rewrite is materially shorter and clearer, ship the rewrite.

## Tone rules

- Write roughly as you would speak to this reader in person; neither chummy with strangers nor
  distant with close colleagues.
- Assume any message may be forwarded, or read aloud in a dispute. Be collegial.
- No sarcasm, ever.
- Never make the reader concede they were wrong; strike "you fail to understand", "you
  erroneously claim". State the facts and the decision.
- Do not write in anger; draft, wait, then revise.

## Channel rules

### Email

Subject line names the action or topic ("Approve Q4 fuel budget by Friday"). The ask is in the
first line. One ask per email; answer all open questions in one reply to avoid ping-pong. Default
under 200 words. Copy only people who must act or know.

### Memos

Every memo has a descriptive title that promises either a status or a recommendation. Address
it to the person who must act. For up to three issues, open with a summary block:

```
Issue:     [the problem in one sentence anyone can understand]
Proposal:  [the recommendation]
Reason:    [why it works or beats the alternatives]
```

The body then gives the detail for fact-checkers and future readers. Recommendations state what,
who, when, where, why and how.

### Letters

Open with the reader's interest, not the writer's. Close with the specific action and date.
For bad news: a genuine positive opening, the reason, the decision, a forward-looking close.
Do not open with the refusal and do not over-explain. For complaints, say please and thank you.

### Fundraising and sales letters

One person writing to one person. Open with the reader's problem or aspiration, not the
organisation. A specific story beats a statistic; a statistic beats an adjective. Offer the
reader a way to meet their own motives (impact, belonging, recognition, duty, relief of worry)
through a worthwhile aim. One specific ask with an amount and a deadline; use the postscript for
the ask because it is heavily read.

### Presentations and speeches

Open with something the audience cannot ignore. Preview, deliver, recap. One idea per slide,
few words per slide, one closing request.

### Plans and reports

Conclusions before history; numbered recommendations with owners and dates; a summary that
stands alone. Structure detail: `../../report-and-proposal-craft/references/report-and-proposal-structure.md`.

### Proposals and recommendations

State the recommendation in the first sentence; frame benefits as what the reader gains, saves
or avoids; answer the obvious objection inside the document.

### Resumes and cover letters

Cover letter tailored to the role and one page. Resume: achievements with numbers, reverse
chronology, one page if possible and two at most; no generic objective statement.

### Factual accounts

Before writing an account of events, list the dated events in order, then turn the list into
prose. Starting mid-story forces the reader to reconstruct the timeline.

### Performance appraisals

Anchor every rating to a named behaviour and its consequence: behaviour, calibrated phrase,
effect. Cover attitude, efficiency, working relationships, judgement, knowledge, reliability and
communication. Unanchored praise and unanchored criticism both fail.

## Self-editing routine

Write first and format later. Revise more than once. Read aloud; where your tongue stumbles, the
reader will too. Aim to cut about a quarter on the first revision. Delete every sentence that can
go without loss. Name files so versions are unambiguous.

## Failure modes

Softened openings and stacked hedges · formal Latinate vocabulary · legal or bureaucratic voice ·
passive voice hiding who decided · inflated adjectives · paragraphs over five sentences · buried
lead · one long email doing three jobs · generic resume objective · a postscript wasted on an
afterthought.

## Original worked example

Before: "Please be advised that, due to the fact that the vendor has not been able to
facilitate delivery, it has been decided that the rollout will be moved going forward."

After: "The Mombasa rollout moves to 14 October because the vendor has not delivered the card
readers. Operations will confirm the new date with branches by Friday."

## Checklist

- [ ] First sentence carries the news.
- [ ] No vague intensifiers; numbers, names and dates instead.
- [ ] Sentences average under 20 words with variety; paragraphs one idea, at most five sentences.
- [ ] Active voice unless the passive is deliberate.
- [ ] Substitution list applied; no bureaucratic words.
- [ ] Tone checked against the relationship and the chance of forwarding.
- [ ] Channel rule applied.
- [ ] Read aloud; names and figures checked.
