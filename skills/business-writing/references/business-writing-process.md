# Business Writing Process

The default workflow for any non-academic prose artifact: email, memo, report, proposal, blog
post, web copy. Sentence-level rules live in `plain-business-prose-and-tone.md`; structural
templates for reports and proposals live in
`../../report-and-proposal-craft/references/report-and-proposal-structure.md`.

## Inputs

- Who reads it (primary, secondary, tertiary) and the decision or action each owns.
- The channel and any length or format constraint.
- The evidence available and what is still missing.
- The deadline, which decides how many revision passes are affordable.

## Decision rules

1. **Write to the decision-maker.** When readers conflict, the person who decides is the primary
   reader. Serve the others with structure (summary, appendix, callout), not with tone.
2. **Commit to three main points before drafting.** Write them as full sentences (subject, verb,
   the logic), in the order the reader needs them, not the order you thought of them. Two feels
   thin; four usually hides a merge. If a genuine fourth appears while drafting, accept it.
3. **Separate generating from judging.** Gathering and brainstorming, organising, drafting, and
   revising are different jobs; doing two at once produces circular rewriting.
4. **Revision is not proofreading.** Revision changes structure and substance; proofreading
   fixes mechanics. Never merge them.
5. **Dense short formats get an expand-and-squeeze loop.** For one-pagers, pitch decks and
   summaries, draft generously, cut hard, restore what proved load-bearing, and cut again before
   the normal revision passes.

## Procedure

| Stage | Output | Gate to the next stage |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Readers and purpose | One sentence: what the reader should think, feel and do afterwards | Readers named; purpose concrete |
| 2. Collect | Source dossier: source, access date, the claim it supports, credibility note | Every intended claim has a source; load-bearing facts have two independent sources |
| 3. Generate | Unfiltered list of 15 to 20 candidate points | List saturated; nothing selected yet |
| 4. Organise | Outline of descriptive headings with one line each | The outline alone narrates the document; sponsor agrees |
| 5. Draft | Complete rough draft with `[TK]` placeholders for missing facts | Whole document exists |
| 6. Revise | Four separate passes: structure, paragraph, sentence, word | Structural problems resolved |
| 7. Proof | Mechanically clean file | No typos, broken links, stale cross-references or wrong metadata |

### Stage notes

- **Readers.** For each reader record role, decision, knowledge, attitude (friendly, neutral,
  hostile) and preferred channel. Writing for yourself, or for the gatekeeper, is the common
  failure.
- **Collect.** Mark verbatim wording and its source at capture time, not while drafting. Pair
  with `../../academic-writing/references/source-away-note-cards.md` when paraphrase risk is high.
- **Generate.** Use opposites ("what if the reverse were true?"), splitting ("is this two
  ideas?") and myth-testing ("which assumption would this kill?").
- **Organise.** Choose the pattern that fits the purpose: chronological, spatial, comparison,
  cause and effect, order of importance, or problem and solution.
- **Draft.** Write fast, in section-sized sittings, with the judging mind switched off.
- **Revise.** Structure pass (move, cut, merge sections); paragraph pass (topic sentence first,
  one idea, explicit transitions); sentence pass (length, voice, parallelism); word pass (cut
  empty modifiers, replace abstractions with specifics).
- **Proof.** On paper, aloud, or paragraph by paragraph from the end. Run spelling, grammar and
  link tools but do not trust them as the last check. Two people read anything that leaves the
  organisation.

## Channel overlays

| Channel | Readers and purpose | Organise | Length | Revision priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Email | One reader, one ask | Ask first, then context | Up to 200 words; subject line carries the verb | Subject line and first sentence |
| Report | Decider plus influencers | Answer first, decimal headings | Per the report guide | Summary stands alone |
| Proposal | The buyer's unspoken objections | Situation, approach, deliverables, price | 8 to 25 pages | Risk and price precision |
| Blog post | The reader's one searchable question | Hook, problem, answer, next step | 800 to 2,000 words | Scannable subheads |
| Web copy | Visitor intent in three seconds | Value first, scannable | Up to 400 words a page | Microcopy and call to action |

## Failure modes

Skipping the reader stage ("my manager will read it") · drafting before outlining · editing
while drafting · combining revision passes · polishing prose that should have been cut · using
the spell-checker as the only proof · an email written as a memo · web copy written as a report ·
a single read-through before sending.

## Premium versus generic output

Generic output starts writing immediately and produces even-length sections that restate the
brief. Senior output shows its three main points in the first screen, has sections of uneven
length because the evidence is uneven, and ends with a specific next action and date.

## Original worked example

A procurement officer at a Kigali hospital must ask the finance director to approve an
emergency oxygen-concentrator purchase.
- Purpose: the director approves RWF 18m today so the supplier can deliver before the weekend.
- Three points: two of five concentrators failed this week; the backup cylinder supply covers
  four days; the only supplier with stock holds the price until 17:00.
- Email subject: "Approve RWF 18m oxygen concentrators today (supplier holds price until 17:00)".
  The ask is the first line; the three points follow; the quotation is attached.

## Checklist

- [ ] Readers named; one-sentence purpose written.
- [ ] Dossier complete; every claim sourced.
- [ ] Three main points written as full sentences.
- [ ] Outline agreed and readable on its own.
- [ ] Full draft with placeholders resolved.
- [ ] Four revision passes done separately.
- [ ] Proofed on paper or aloud; second reader for external documents.
