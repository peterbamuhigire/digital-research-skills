## What the books add to the existing standard

The existing five-pass human-English standard remains the controlling editorial
method. This overlay makes one part of its sentence pass more explicit:

1. **Choose the meaning before the ornament.** A synonym is not interchangeable merely because a list places it nearby. Check sense, connotation, grammatical behaviour, audience, and register.
2. **Check the word partnership.** Natural English depends on combinations, not isolated dictionary words. Check the verb, noun, adjective, adverb, preposition, and grammatical frame together.
3. **Prefer an exact ordinary word.** Advanced vocabulary is available when it is the most accurate choice, not because it sounds educated. Do not make prose harder to prove, translate, skim, or act on.
4. **Label register in the writer's mind.** A phrase may be neutral, formal, conversational, legal, journalistic, technical, local, dated, or metaphorical. Match the reader and medium.
5. **Use intensity as a claim.** Words such as substantial, deeply, sharply, widespread, robust, leading, or outstanding increase the burden of evidence. Replace them with a fact, comparison, mechanism, boundary, or calibrated qualifier where evidence is limited.
6. **Treat idioms as a deliberate tool.** An idiom must be understood by the intended audience, fit the genre, survive translation and accessibility needs, and add meaning. In requirements, legal, financial, operational, safety, UI, and international technical writing, the literal alternative normally wins.
7. **Proof form as well as meaning.** Recheck spelling, homophones, agreement, articles, tense/aspect, pronoun reference, prepositions, parallelism, modifiers, punctuation, and sentence boundaries. A memorised exam answer is not proof of current usage.
8. **Let context decide.** Topic lists are prompts for discovery, not copy to paste. Search the local corpus, approved glossary, current dictionary, client terminology, or authoritative domain source when a phrase is uncertain.

## House quality: polished, not pompous

The requested Eton-level quality is interpreted as polished, literate, exact,
well-read, and restrained. It does not mean archaic diction, social imitation,
ornamental sentences, or imported British mannerisms. The writer should sound like
a highly educated professional who understands the reader's time and stakes.

Use:

- concrete nouns, active and accountable verbs, and clean sentence architecture;
- varied rhythm that follows the movement of the thought;
- courteous confidence without flattery, theatre, or false certainty;
- precise local and institutional terms when they are real and approved; and
- a human judgement, example, trade-off, or implication where the material warrants it.

Reject:

- generic openings, inflated claims, automatic three-adjective piles, and repeated paragraph moulds;
- thesaurus substitutions that change the sense or sound unnatural;
- forced idioms, pseudo-literary metaphors, fake warmth, manufactured slang, and deliberate errors;
- jargon used to perform intelligence rather than carry a necessary distinction; and
- polished language that conceals an evidence gap, missing owner, weak mechanism, or untested assumption.

## Collocation and lexical decision procedure

For every material sentence, especially a heading, claim, CTA, requirement,
recommendation, or customer-facing message:

1. State the intended meaning in plain words.
2. Select the simplest accurate word or phrase.
3. Check its natural grammatical frame and nearby word choices.
4. Check connotation and register against the reader, channel, locale, and purpose.
5. Check whether the phrase makes a stronger factual claim than the evidence supports.
6. Keep the established product, client, legal, accounting, technical, and proper-name terminology.
7. Search a current authoritative dictionary, corpus, or house glossary if usage is uncertain.
8. Read the sentence aloud, then proof it in context rather than as an isolated word.

Original examples of the principle include: **make a contribution**, **carry out a
survey**, **conduct research**, **reach an agreement**, **provide evidence**, and
**address a problem**. These are prompts to check the frame, not a list to force
into every document. For technical and regulated work, the domain's approved term
overrides a general-language preference.

## Default locale and exceptions

The default is British English with a neutral East African professional register
where the brief is for Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania, or a comparable regional audience.
The actual audience or buyer instruction overrides that default. Record the chosen
locale, spelling convention, date convention, currency and institutional names.
Do not rewrite an official name, quotation, product label, statute, standard title,
or API identifier for style.

East African warmth must be genuine and proportionate. Courtesy is welcome; repeated
"kindly", indirectness that hides responsibility, or regionally familiar phrasing
that a wider audience may misread is not a quality requirement. When the audience is
international or multilingual, favour literal, translatable, accessible English.

## Five-pass release overlay

The owning engine still runs its domain gate. Add these checks to its language
review:

1. **Brief:** reader, purpose, locale, genre, channel, and next action are named.
2. **Meaning:** each claim is separated from evidence, inference, recommendation, assumption, and promise.
3. **Language:** collocation, grammar, spelling, register, terminology, and idiom fit are checked.
4. **Human revision:** generic phrasing, repeated structures, inflated adjectives, and unsupported certainty are cut or made specific.
5. **Proof:** names, figures, dates, links, identifiers, accessibility text, and domain terms are checked; the final text is read aloud where practical.
